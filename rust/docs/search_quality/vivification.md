# Vivification of learnt clauses: does shortening the learnt clauses pay?

`src/topi_ext_vivify.rs` (off by default) adds a vivification round to inprocessing: at decision level 0 the solver takes a learnt clause off its watches, decides the negations
of its literals one by one with BCP in between, and replaces the clause by the subset of its literals, which a contradiction, an implied literal of the clause, or the literals falsified
by propagation leave (the "vivification: see Kissat paper" of the C++ to-do list). The clauses are tried in the order of increasing glue and decreasing activity, a clause is tried once,
and a round may propagate `/inprocessing/vivify/effort` (0.1) times the literals the search has propagated since the previous round.

    /inprocessing/on 1 /inprocessing/conflicts_before_next 5000 /inprocessing/vivify/on 1

runs a round at the start of every query and at the first restart after every 5000 conflicts (the schedule is that of the existing inprocessing: without `conflicts_before_next`
there is one round per query, before its first decision).

Search quality is measured in **conflicts** (cumulative over the queries of an instance; those of the search: a round counts neither conflicts nor decisions); CPU time is secondary,
but it is where vivification pays its bill, so it is reported next to the conflicts.
The full tables are in [`vivification_tables.md`](vivification_tables.md), generated by `scripts/vivify_bench.py` (2016 runs: 14 configurations over 144 instances, 120 s timeout, 57 runs without an answer; 3.6 hours of CPU time, about 50 minutes with 6 jobs).
Every model was verified by the tool, and no run contradicted another one (SAT versus UNSAT).

## The instances, and what they can show

* The 78 regression instances. 74 of them are trivial (383 conflicts in total): no round runs on them, and their conflicts are unchanged.
* The four recorded `planner_*` instances and their three derived versions each (`.nolf`, `.lf`, `.plain`, see [`ceiling.md`](ceiling.md)). Their conflict counts are very sensitive to *any* change
  of the search, so they are read against a control.
* Generated formulas, since nothing above is a hard query. Structured ones: factoring by an array multiplier (a semiprime of two 20- or 21-bit primes is satisfiable: 12 instances;
  a prime of 33 to 37 bits is unsatisfiable: 12 instances, 58 to 212 thousand conflicts each), 3-colouring of random graphs at the threshold (400 vertices: 6 unsatisfiable, 2 satisfiable; 600 vertices below it: satisfiable),
  two pigeonhole formulas; and uniform random 3-SAT at the threshold (250 variables: 7 satisfiable, 5 unsatisfiable).
* **Two controls.** `ing` is the inprocessing schedule without vivification: it reproduces the conflicts of the baseline on every instance, so whatever changes is due to vivification.
  `flip1000` (`/decision/polarity/flip_factor 1000`, a parameter of the C++ solver, which flips every 1000th polarity selection) is a perturbation without information:
  a gain is believed only where it is consistent over the instances and the perturbation doesn't show it.

## Reading

1. **Refutations of structured formulas get shorter, consistently.** On the 12 factoring refutations vivification saves 17% of the conflicts and 13% of the CPU time: 9 to 27% of the conflicts and 3 to 29% of the time per instance,
   so every one of the 12 is better in both (the larger the instance, the more: 0.86, 0.83, 0.81 of the conflicts for 17, 18 and 19 bits). The conflicts are fewer on all the 12 in both restart modes
   and at every effort and schedule tried but one run, while the control changes nothing (1.02, 4 better and 8 worse).
   This is the effect vivification is known for: a shorter learnt clause propagates earlier and makes the later learnt clauses shorter.
   On the other unsatisfiable families the gain is within what the control shows (3-colouring: 0.96 versus 0.97; pigeonhole: 0.94 versus 0.90), so over all the 20 structured refutations
   the claim is 12% fewer conflicts and 6% less CPU time, most of it from one family.
2. **Nothing can be claimed for satisfiable queries.** Their conflict counts move by tens of percent with any perturbation (the control: 1.37 on the structured ones, 0.74 on the random ones),
   and vivification moves them the same way in both directions (1.14 at the effort 0.1, 0.85 at 0.3, 1.33 at 0.03). It neither finds models faster nor slower in a way these 26 instances can show.
3. **The planner workload shows no gain.** The recorded instances take 1.13 of the conflicts (worse on all the three, though by less than the control's 1.24) and 1.27 of the CPU time (1.18 in an earlier run of the same table: these runs take a fraction of a second, so their times are noisy); without the planner's polarities (`.nolf`)
   they take 0.78 (better on all the three), which says more about the sensitivity of these instances than about vivification: the same three instances are 1.00 at the efforts 0.03 and 0.3.
   Only one clause in seven is shortened there, by one or two literals: the planner's queries are short, and their learnt clauses are already tight.
4. **The effort is paid in CPU time at a worse rate than budgeted.** The budget counts propagated literals, and a literal propagated at the top of an almost empty trail visits longer watch lists than one in the search:
   on random 3-SAT refutations, where the conflicts don't change (1.01), 10% more propagations cost 34% more CPU time, and the effort 1 costs 3.1 times the time; on the structured refutations the overhead is about 7% at the effort 0.1
   (0.939 of the time for 0.878 of the conflicts). So the conflicts keep improving with the effort (0.91, 0.88, 0.84, 0.81 for 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1), and the time is best at 0.03 to 0.1 and lost from 0.3 on.
5. **The schedule and the glue bound matter little.** A round every 1000, 5000 or 20000 conflicts gives 0.87, 0.88, 0.90 of the conflicts on the structured refutations; with the budget of 0.1 a round rarely gets
   beyond the clauses of a low glue anyway, so `max_glue 6` changes almost nothing, and `max_glue 2` is as good in conflicts (0.889) and slightly cheaper (0.917 of the time).
6. **What was tried and dropped.** The order, in which the literals of a clause are decided: besides the stored order (the one implemented), an exploratory run tried the number of occurrences
   in the candidates (as CaDiCaL does), the decision score, and the reversed order. Its differences were within the noise of the satisfiable instances, so the simplest order stayed;
   the code of the alternatives is not in the tree, so `scripts/vivify_bench.py` doesn't reproduce that comparison, and no numbers are claimed for it.

## Recommended setting

* **Default: off** (`/inprocessing/vivify/on 0`), as every Rust-only extension: the search, the output and the proofs are those of the C++ solver.
* **For long, refutation-heavy queries over structured formulas** (tens of thousands of conflicts and more; equivalence checking, arithmetic, anything where the answer is expected to be UNSAT):

      /inprocessing/on 1 /inprocessing/conflicts_before_next 5000 /inprocessing/vivify/on 1

  with the default effort 0.1: about 12% fewer conflicts and 6% less CPU time on the structured refutations here (17% and 13% on the factoring ones). If CPU time matters more than conflicts,
  or the formulas may be random-like, add `/inprocessing/vivify/effort 0.03`: 9% fewer conflicts and 10% less time on the structured refutations, and 6% of the time lost on the random 3-SAT ones, where vivification doesn't help.
  Efforts of 0.3 and above buy conflicts with time and are not recommended.
* **For the planner workload and for satisfiable queries in general: leave it off.** No gain is measurable on them, and the rounds cost time (the recorded planner instances: 1.13 of the conflicts and 1.27 of the CPU time).

## Limits of the implementation

* Only learnt clauses are vivified (the clauses of the user are never touched), a clause is tried once (a clause, which was shortened, is a new clause and is tried again), and the trail is not reused
  between two clauses (CaDiCaL and Kissat sort the candidates, so that consecutive ones share decisions); the budget is measured in propagated literals, not in memory accesses (see 4 above).
* A round runs at decision level 0: the assumptions are taken off the trail and propagated again after the round, which is a fixed cost per round for queries under many assumptions.
* The DRAT proof gets a `d` line per replaced clause, right after the line of the clause which replaces it; these are the only deletions the solver writes.
  The proofs of 13 unsatisfiable instances (factoring, colouring, pigeonhole, random) in the three solver modes, textual and binary, with a round every 2000 conflicts at the effort 0.3
  (1.5 to 17 thousand replaced clauses per proof) were verified by `drat-trim`: 78 of 78.
