# Target/best-phase saving and rephasing: does the self-generated phase oracle help?

[`ceiling.md`](ceiling.md) measured what phase advice can buy at best, with the advice taken from the answer. This experiment measures the advice the solver can generate for itself,
as CaDiCaL and Kissat do (`src/topi_ext_target_phase.rs`, off by default):

* **Target phases** (`/phase/target/on 1`): the assignment at the largest conflict-free trail since the latest rephase; a decision takes the target phase in preference to the saved phase.
* **Rephasing** (`/phase/rephase/conf_init N`, `conf_incr`, `conf_mult`, `random`): on a conflict schedule, at a restart, the saved phases are reset to the original, inverted, best or flipped ones
  (O I (B F B O B I)*), and the target is reset.

Search quality is measured in **conflicts** (cumulative over the queries of an instance); decisions and CPU time are secondary.
The full tables are in [`target_phase_tables.md`](target_phase_tables.md), generated by `scripts/target_phase_bench.py` (1820 runs: 13 configurations over 140 instances, 60 s timeout; under 10 minutes with 6 jobs).
Every model was verified by the tool, and no run contradicted another one (SAT versus UNSAT). The conflict counts are deterministic: a second run of the script reproduced every one of them
(only the runs at the edge of the timeout change sides).

## The instances, and what they can show

* The 78 regression instances. 74 of them are trivial (383 conflicts in total), and the extension changes one conflict among them (383 -> 384), so they show nothing either way.
* The four recorded `planner_*` instances and their three derived versions each (`.nolf`: the recorded queries without the `lf` commands; `.lf`, `.plain`: a single query with and without them);
  `planner_constraint_cores` is trivial in every version. As `ceiling.md` found, their conflict counts are very sensitive to *any* change of the decisions,
  so they are read against a control here too.
* Generated formulas, since nothing above is a hard query: uniform random 3-SAT at the threshold (200 variables, ratio 4.26: 15 satisfiable, 9 unsatisfiable),
  below it (250 variables: 16, and 350 variables: 8, ratio 4.0; all satisfiable, the larger ones hard for CDCL: the baseline takes 100 to 550 thousand conflicts for 6 of the 8 and doesn't finish one), and two pigeonhole formulas.
* **The control** is `flip1000` (`/decision/polarity/flip_factor 1000`, a parameter of the C++ solver, which flips every 1000th polarity selection): a perturbation of the phases without information.
  A gain is believed only where the control doesn't show it.

## Reading

1. **On hard satisfiable formulas the gain is large, and it is not a perturbation effect.** With target phases and rephasing every generated satisfiable formula is solved, the 350-variable ones
   in 5 to 32 thousand conflicts, where the baseline takes 99 to 551 thousand for six of them and times out on one (0.08 of the conflicts over the seven it decides; CPU: 2.5 to 41 s -> under 0.5 s for those six).
   The control gains nothing over the 39 satisfiable formulas (0.92, 15 wins to 20 losses, and it loses three of the 350-variable formulas to the timeout), while target + rephase takes 0.44 (28 wins to 10).
   The gain grows with the hardness: 0.82 at 200 variables, 0.52 at 250, 0.08 at 350.
2. **It is a mean over a wide spread, with real regressions on single instances.** `rand3sat_n350_r4.0_s5`: 6093 -> 32463 conflicts (the one loss of the group; the baseline was lucky);
   `rand3sat_n250_r4.0_s2`: 999 -> 10177 to 78194, depending on the schedule; at the threshold (n=200) 6 of the 15 satisfiable formulas get worse. A single run says little; the direction over a family does.
3. **Refutations get slower, mildly but consistently.** All 9 unsatisfiable random formulas take more conflicts with target phases (1.11 to 1.12 on average, at most 1.21: 36385 -> 44062), and 1.13 in CPU time over the unsatisfiable formulas.
   This is the expected price: the target phases steer towards a model, which doesn't exist. Rephasing alone costs less (1.06), and the pigeonhole formulas gain from it (0.71 to 0.89; two instances).
4. **The planner workload doesn't gain, as `ceiling.md` predicted.** On the recorded instances the extension is within the noise, and on the wrong side of it: 1.23 with target + rephase, where the control is at 1.24;
   `planner_small_refinements` goes from 6724 to 12125 conflicts (the control: 9783). The planner fixes the polarities of practically every variable with `lf`, which wins over the target phase,
   and its queries are refutations and exhausted budgets. The single-query `.lf` versions get worse in the default mode (1.57; the control: 1.30) and better in the mode 1 (0.37, 3 of 3),
   the `.plain` ones better (0.49; the control: 0.58): these are the final queries, which `ceiling.md` showed to be solved by any change of the decision order, so none of this is evidence.
   Rephasing alone is neutral on the recorded, `.nolf` and `.lf` planner instances (0.94 to 1.03).
5. **The schedule matters less than the noise.** 1000, 300, geometric and random-instead-of-flipped are within each other's spread over all the satisfiable formulas (0.43 to 0.51); rephasing every 100 conflicts is the weakest (0.60),
   and the short intervals lose at the threshold (1.25 and 1.13 at n=200). Target phases *without* rephasing have the best mean here (0.39) and the worst refutations (1.09, 0 of 11);
   without a rephase the target is never reset, which these small formulas don't punish and a long run would.
6. **Under LBD-based restarts (the mode 1)** the picture is the same (0.54 on the satisfiable formulas, 1.19 on the unsatisfiable ones). `/phase/target/on 2` uses no target phases there, by design,
   so it is rephasing alone: no gain on the satisfiable formulas (0.93), and 0.94 on the refutations (9 of 11 improve). The mode 1 itself takes more conflicts than the mode 0 on these formulas, so this is not a reason to switch modes.
7. **Decisions and time** follow the conflicts (decisions: 0.54 / 1.06, CPU: 0.49 / 1.13 for target + rephase 1000 over the satisfiable / unsatisfiable instances). The bookkeeping is one pass over the variables whenever the
   conflict-free trail reaches a new maximum, and one per query and per rephase.

## Recommended setting

* **Leave the extension off by default** (it is, and with it off the solver is bit-identical to the C++ one), and **off for the planner workload**: no gain was measured there, and refutations pay about 5 to 12% more conflicts.
* **For a hard query, which is expected to be satisfiable: `/phase/target/on 1 /phase/rephase/conf_init 1000`** (with the default `/phase/rephase/conf_incr 1000` and `conf_mult 1`: the schedule of CaDiCaL).
  It is the most consistent setting measured (28 wins to 10, no formula lost to the timeout, 0.44 of the conflicts), and the alternatives are not distinguishable from it on this data.
* If the answer is unknown and refutations matter, `/phase/rephase/conf_init 1000` alone is the cautious choice: 0.55 on the satisfiable formulas (but it lost two of the 350-variable ones to the timeout) and 1.02 on the unsatisfiable ones.

## Limits of the evidence

* The satisfiable formulas, on which the gain shows, are uniform random 3-SAT: the family where target phases are known to help most. There is no hard *structured* satisfiable instance in the repository
  (the regression instances are trivial, the planner's hard queries are not satisfiable), so the size of the gain on such instances is unmeasured.
* One run per instance and configuration; the spread between formulas stands in for seeds. The 350-variable group has 8 formulas, the pigeonhole one 2, the planner ones 3 each.
* No alternation of the restart strategies within a query (the stable/focused modes of CaDiCaL) is implemented: `/phase/target/on 2` only restricts the target phases to the queries, whose strategy is the numeric one.
